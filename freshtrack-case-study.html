Design Case Study
Kitchen inventory & expiry tracker

FreshTrack

A pantry app that keeps track of what's actually in the kitchen, so food gets used before it goes off instead of after.

RoleSolo product & UI designer
ForA friend's personal project
ToolFigma
StatusPrototype, not yet built
The Problem

Nobody opens the fridge expecting a surprise.

Most kitchens run on memory, and memory is bad at expiry dates.

FreshTrack came out of a plain, ordinary frustration: things go bad at the back of the fridge or pantry without anyone noticing, the same items get bought twice because nobody remembered what was already there, and there's no easy record of what's actually on hand versus what's just assumed to be there.

None of that needed a market study to identify. It's the kind of problem you notice by living with it, which is also its limit as a design brief.

Honesty note: this project is grounded in personal experience and everyday observation, not formal user research or interviews. It's a first design pass, not a validated one.
What it needed to do

Three jobs, in order of how often they actually happen.

  • 01

    Make logging an item almost free. Any inventory app dies the moment logging feels like a chore — the first design problem was friction, not features.

  • 02

    Surface what's about to expire before it's wasted. A silent list is useless. The app needed to put urgency in front of the person, unprompted.

  • 03

    Turn "running low" into "add to the list." Tracking a pantry is only worth the effort if it makes the next shopping trip smarter.

Approach

Designing from the kitchen, not from a persona doc.

This was a solo project, designed in Figma with no team and no formal research phase. Without interviews to lean on, the safest move was to design against a specific, real routine — how groceries actually get put away, forgotten, and thrown out — rather than inventing a generic "busy home cook" persona that could justify almost any feature.

That constraint shaped the priority order above: entry friction got solved first, because it's the one thing that would kill the app before the expiry tracking ever got a chance to matter.

The Flow

Walking through it the way it actually gets used.

Five screens, in the order a real session usually runs: check the kitchen, log something new, browse what's there, shop for what's missing, and manage the account underneath it all.

1
Home — "My Kitchen"

Lead with urgency, not inventory count.

The home screen doesn't open with a list — it opens with three numbers: total items, expired, and expiring soon, followed by a "Use First" section. The instinct with an inventory app is to show the inventory first. Here the more useful opening question is narrower: what needs attention right now?

When nothing is expiring, the screen says so plainly ("You're doing great!") instead of showing an empty state that reads like a bug.

2
Add Item

One task, three ways in.

Scan, Manual, and Voice sit side by side at the top, because different moments call for different amounts of effort — a manual entry for control, a barcode scan for a packaged item, voice for when both hands are full of groceries.

Below that: category chips, quantity and unit, an expiry date field, and an "Add Batch" option for when the same item comes home with more than one expiry date — two milk cartons bought a week apart, for instance. Storage location (Fridge, Freezer, Pantry, Cabinet) is its own field, since where something lives changes how long it lasts.

3
Pantry

The browsing view, separate from the triage view.

The dashboard answers "what needs attention." The Pantry tab answers "what do I actually have" — a searchable, filterable, full list, useful mid-cook or mid-shop rather than at a glance. Each row carries a countdown badge ("5 days left") so status is visible without opening the item, and edit or remove sits directly on the row instead of behind a menu.

4
Shopping

Where tracking pays off.

This is the screen the rest of the app exists to feed. Suggestions are pulled from pantry state — what's run out or is running low — rather than kept as a separate list someone has to maintain by hand. A tap moves a suggestion onto the actual list; a plain "Add custom item" field covers anything the logic can't guess.

5
Profile

Kept small on purpose.

Personal Information, Notifications, Privacy & Security, App Settings — four rows, nothing more. Notifications get their own row rather than being buried in settings, since reminders are the core promise of the app. Guest access means someone can start logging their pantry before ever creating an account.

Key Decisions

Four calls that shaped the rest of the design.

Decision 01

Three entry paths, not one

Logging is the one habit the whole app depends on. Giving it three doors — scan, type, speak — was worth the extra design surface.

Decision 02

Color over dates

A raw date makes someone do math. A green "5 days left" badge doesn't — urgency is legible before the number is even read.

Decision 03

"Use First," not a full list, on Home

The home screen answers one question well instead of every question shallowly. Full inventory has its own tab.

Decision 04

Shopping list generated, not written

Suggestions come from pantry state so the app closes the loop it opened, instead of being two disconnected tools in one shell.

Where It Stands

Prototype, not product.

FreshTrack exists today as a Figma prototype. It hasn't been built, and it hasn't been tested with anyone outside the design process. The honest next steps:

  • Put the prototype in front of the friend it was designed for, and watch where the logging flow actually breaks down.
  • Pressure-test the "Use First" logic — right now it's a design assumption about what counts as urgent, not a validated rule.
  • Decide whether barcode scanning is feasible to build first, since it's the entry path doing the most work to reduce friction.
