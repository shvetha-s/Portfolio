FreshTrack — Case Study
Design Case Study

FreshTrack

A pantry app that keeps track of what's actually in the kitchen, so food gets used before it goes off instead of after.

Role Solo product & UI designer
For Personal project
Tool Figma
Status Prototype, not yet built
The Problem

Nobody opens the fridge expecting a surprise.

Most kitchens run on memory, and memory is bad at expiry dates.

FreshTrack came out of a plain, ordinary frustration: things go bad at the back of the fridge or pantry without anyone noticing, the same items get bought twice because nobody remembered what was already there, and there's no easy record of what's actually on hand versus what's just assumed to be there.

None of that needed a market study to identify. It's the kind of problem you notice by living with it, which is also its limit as a design brief.

Honesty note: This project is grounded in personal experience and everyday observation, not formal user research or interviews. It's a first design pass, not a validated one.
What it needed to do

Three jobs, in order of how often they actually happen.

01
Make logging an item almost free. Any inventory app dies the moment logging feels like a chore — the first design problem was friction, not features.
02
Surface what's about to expire before it's wasted. A silent list is useless. The app needed to put urgency in front of the person, unprompted.
03
Turn "running low" into "add to the list." Tracking a pantry is only worth the effort if it makes the next shopping trip smarter.
Approach

Designing from the kitchen, not from a persona doc.

This was a solo project, designed in Figma with no team and no formal research phase. Without interviews to lean on, the safest move was to design against a specific, real routine — how groceries actually get put away, forgotten, and thrown out — rather than inventing a generic "busy home cook" persona that could justify almost any feature.

That constraint shaped the priority order above: entry friction got solved first, because it's the one thing that would kill the app before the expiry tracking ever got a chance to matter.

The Flow

Walking through it the way it actually gets used.

Five screens, in the order a real session usually runs: check the kitchen, log something new, browse what's there, shop for what's missing, and manage the account underneath it all.
